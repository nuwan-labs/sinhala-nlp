BLOCK 1 - REPLACEMENT FOR: Research Problem (whole section)
================================================================================
NEW TEXT:

Sri Lankan traditional medicine has been written in Sinhala for centuries. These
texts record herbal formulas in a regular form: the ingredients of a remedy, how
it is prepared, what it treats, the dose, and the substance it is taken with. The
Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of Sri Lanka, published by the Department of Ayurveda, is
one such source; its formula section is laid out as structured entries with
reference lists of plant names, disease names, minerals, and units, which makes
it a good starting point for studying the language the formulas are written in.
The section holds roughly 700 formulas over about 60,600 running words, each
naming on average some fifteen ingredients, so the material is small but densely
structured.

Sinhala is one of the lowest-resource languages in natural language processing
(Joshi et al., 2020), and falls outside the main body of Indic work, since the
AI4Bharat models cover the scheduled languages of India and leave Sinhala out
(Gala et al., 2023). Its dependency treebank has only one hundred sentences
(Liyanage et al., 2023), and the pre-trained models that exist were trained on
news and web text rather than any specialised domain (Dhananjaya et al., 2022).
The formula texts add further difficulty. They are written in a specialised
register that draws on several strata, everyday Sinhala alongside a large
Sanskrit-derived vocabulary and, more thinly, Tamil and Arabic terms: about four
fifths of the ingredient names and three fifths of the formula-name words resolve
to a Sanskrit lemma, yet they are written in Sinhala script and set in ordinary
Sinhala grammar. The script itself is an abugida whose joined consonants are
formed with a special joiner, so a single word costs on the order of thirteen
bytes and fragments badly under byte and codepoint units. The ingredient names
form a large open class while the indications reduce to a few dozen recurring
concepts, and more than half of all word types occur only once. On top of this
the printed pages reach the computer through optical character recognition, whose
error, measured here against a second engine, runs near nine percent of
characters and mints spurious word types if left uncontrolled.

These texts raise four connected questions, set out below: whether the register
is restricted in the way a sublanguage is (Grishman and Kittredge, 1986), how an
abugida like Sinhala should be split into units for it (Zouhar et al., 2023),
whether its structure, once described, predicts how hard its content is to
extract, and whether its Sanskrit-derived layer carries any advantage for a model
already trained on Sanskrit (Nehrdich et al., 2024).

NEW TEXT:

The data is the structured formula section of the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of Sri
Lanka: roughly 700 distinct formulas after removing page-continuation fragments
and duplicates, spanning pages 172 to 443, about 60,600 running words with a
vocabulary of some 7,000 word types. The corpus is small, short-form, and
lexically sparse: a formula runs to about 87 words and names some fifteen
ingredients, and more than half of all word types occur only once. Each formula
is organised by six fields, name, constituents, preparation, indication, dosage,
and accompanying substance, the first three almost always present and the rest in
roughly half to two thirds. Two properties set the register apart from the
general-domain Sinhala on which existing tools are trained. Its ingredient names
form a large open class of several hundred distinct concepts, while its
indications reduce to a few dozen closed concepts, and the two share almost no
vocabulary; this asymmetry drives the extraction work. And its technical layer is
Sanskrit-derived but written in Sinhala script and inflected as everyday Sinhala,
about four fifths of the ingredient names resolving to a Sanskrit lemma, so a
typical indication reads as a run of Sanskrit disease terms closed by an ordinary
Sinhala verb, and the preparation prose runs as chains of non-finite step verbs,
grind, strain, boil down, dry, and bind, closed by a verb of obligation. The
abugida's joined consonants make the script costly to segment, near thirteen
bytes to the word, and optical character recognition adds error near nine percent
of characters. The four components proceed in order, each consuming the previous
output.

BLOCK 4 - REPLACEMENT FOR: the characterisation paragraph in Research Methodology
  (the paragraph beginning "Register characterisation tests whether ...")
================================================================================
NEW TEXT:

Register characterisation tests whether the formula language is restricted in the
way a sublanguage is (Grishman and Kittredge, 1986). Because lexical-richness
measures vary with sample size (Baayen, 2001), vocabulary growth is fitted under a
Large-Number-of-Rare-Events model and compared against general-domain Sinhala
baselines reduced to the register's size by binomial interpolation rather than
upward extrapolation (Evert and Baroni, 2007), reporting Baayen's productivity
index as the primary statistic (Baayen, 1996) and lexical and morphological
closure alongside, following an account already shown to transfer to a non-Latin
clinical register (Temnikova et al., 2014). Constructional restriction is measured
on two recurring constructions the register makes visible, the clause-final
predicates that close indication clauses and the chains of non-finite step verbs
that build the preparation prose, each reported against a general-domain baseline
at matched count so that generic high-frequency endings do not stand in for
restriction. Semantic-class separation is measured above what the field layout
imposes. Each estimate carries a resampled interval and is recomputed on a cleaned
sample to discount recognition error.
